Report and Recommendations In Brief
The UK Covid-19 Inquiry is an independent public inquiry examining the response to, and
impact of, the Covid-19 pandemic to learn lessons for the future. Its terms of reference were
set by the then Prime Minister Boris Johnson.

The scale of the pandemic was unprecedented; the Inquiry has a huge range of issues to
cover. The Chair of the Inquiry, Baroness (Heather) Hallett DBE, decided to address this
challenge by dividing its work into separate investigations known as modules. Each module
focuses on a different topic with its own public hearings where the Chair heard evidence.

Following hearings, recommendations for change are developed and put into a Module
Report. Each report contains findings from the evidence collected in that module and the
Chair’s recommendations for the future. Reports for Modules 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 have already
been published.

The seventh module examines the testing, tracing and isolation systems of the UK as it
entered the Covid-19 pandemic and the measures subsequently adopted in England, Wales,
Scotland and Northern Ireland from January 2020 until 28 June 2022. It looks at the
decisions made by each nation’s government and considers:

•    how test, trace and isolate measures were developed and deployed;

•    how the test, trace and isolate system was structured, including the use of the private
     sector and other institutions;

•    how well various testing, tracing and isolation policies, technologies and digital tools
     worked; and

•    what helped or undermined public compliance with isolation rules, including messaging,
     trust in government, financial support and enforcement.

The module also considers the need to build on the infrastructure, staff capacity and
research capability available in the UK so that the UK is better prepared for future
pandemics.

Future reports will focus on the following areas:

•    Module 8 - Children and young people

•    Module 9 - The economic response to the pandemic

•    Module 10 - The impact on society

Module 7: Test, trace and isolate
The UK entered the Covid-19 pandemic underprepared to deliver test, trace and isolate
measures quickly and at a large scale. While parts of these systems were set up quickly and
worked well, overall they came too late and at a huge financial cost. The Inquiry found major
weaknesses in how the country prepared and delivered its response.

Building the test and trace systems capable of delivering at scale cost an extraordinary
amount of money. The UK government spent more than £25 billion on NHS Test and Trace
in England between the start of the pandemic and June 2022. Better planning - with clearer
requirements, proper evaluation and earlier diagnostic testing - is needed. This should
reduce the cost and ensure the UK has effective systems in place to identify infected
people and better control transmission of a virus at a much earlier stage in the next
pandemic.

Although UK scientific capability quickly developed a reliable test by January 2020, testing
the population proved more difficult. Existing infrastructure could not be developed fast
enough to meet high demand, and widespread community testing was paused in March
2020. The pause in widespread contact tracing temporarily removed an important tool for
identifying potentially infected people across the country. This came at a time when the
virus was spreading rapidly, leaving decision-makers to manage the pandemic with limited
information about cases, transmission and the range of symptoms. To be ready for future
pandemics, the UK must protect its research and clinical networks and build on existing
infrastructure so that testing and tracing can expand quickly without losing vital time in a
crisis.

Testing and tracing only works if people who test positive can stay away from others. While
isolating people with Covid-19 was central to the response, the public was initially given
little practical or financial help to do so. This lack of support made it harder for people to
follow the rules; relying on personal responsibility was not enough. These difficulties were
foreseeable, yet planning for support fell short - a mistake which must not be repeated in
planning for future pandemics.

 Key findings
•    The UK was severely underprepared to deliver widespread testing, tracing and isolation
     at the reach and scale needed in a pandemic.

•    Despite showing world-class scientific capability by developing a test in January 2020,
     the UK lacked co-ordinated laboratory capacity to deliver testing. This led to a pause in
     community testing by March 2020 and left decision-makers with limited data on cases,
     transmission and symptoms.

•    Manual contact tracing capacity was quickly overwhelmed and temporarily stopped as
     case numbers rose during the first wave. Later, digital apps helped. However, they took
     time to develop and had accessibility challenges, particularly for digitally excluded
     groups and some minority communities, where factors such as trust, language barriers
     and literacy levels affected engagement. Some people asked to self-isolate initially had
     limited financial and practical support. This undermined compliance.

•    Despite years of public health emergency planning and pandemic preparedness
     exercises, when Covid-19 emerged in January 2020, the UK government and devolved
     administrations did not have effective systems in place for widespread testing, tracing or
     isolation.

•    While the UK eventually constructed a highly successful and widespread test, trace and
     isolate network, the fact that building such a vast operation started almost from scratch
     and during a crisis meant it arrived too late.

•    Governments across all four nations lacked reliable ways to check if policies were
     working, or if people were complying with isolation rules and guidance.

•    The UK lacked a clear strategy for implementing international border control measures
     early in the pandemic, a critical gap in the UK’s ability to limit the spread of the virus.

•    Future planning must build upon existing infrastructure so testing and tracing can scale
     rapidly without delay. Fundamental to this is the development of a single UK-wide test,
     trace and isolate system. The system should be accessible and include clear
     communication, border strategies and guaranteed financial support to ensure future
     schemes are fair, cost-effective and deliverable.

Recommendations
To ensure that the UK is better prepared to identify and control transmission in a future
public health emergency, this Report makes nine recommendations. In summary, these are:


     monitoring the UK’s testing capabilities to ensure that the diagnostics sector is used
     effectively;


     ensuring that plans are in place to deliver manual contact tracing on a large scale;


     developing a UK-wide digital contact tracing tool;


     ensuring that local plans are in place to deliver practical support to communities if
     self-isolation is required;

     ensuring that plans are in place for financial support if self-isolation is needed;


     planning for the use of international border control measures;


     developing aligned communications campaigns about testing, tracing and isolation
     to avoid confusion or ambiguity;


     improving data collection and evaluation of testing, tracing and isolation so that the
     impact on the public and levels of adherence can be monitored effectively; and


     producing a single UK-wide system for testing, tracing and isolation.



A full list of the Inquiry’s recommendations is included in Appendix 3 of the full report. The
Chair expects that all recommendations are acted upon and implemented. The Inquiry will
monitor the implementation of the recommendations during its lifetime.
