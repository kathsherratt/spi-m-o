Report and Recommendations In Brief
The UK Covid-19 Inquiry is an independent public inquiry examining the response to, and
impact of, the Covid-19 pandemic to learn lessons for the future. Its terms of reference were
set by the then Prime Minister Boris Johnson.

The scale of the pandemic was unprecedented; the Inquiry has a huge range of issues to
cover. The Chair of the Inquiry, Baroness (Heather) Hallett DBE, decided to address this
challenge by dividing its work into separate investigations known as modules. Each module
focuses on a different topic with its own public hearings where the Chair heard evidence.

Following hearings, recommendations for change are developed and put into a Module
Report. Each report contains findings from the evidence collected in that module and the
Chair’s recommendations for the future. Reports for Modules 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 have already
been published.

The sixth module examines the impact of the pandemic on the adult social care sector in
England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.1 The module includes care provided in adult
care and residential homes, in people’s own homes by professional carers and unpaid care
provided by family members or friends. It looks at the decisions made by each nation’s
government and considers:

•    what happened to people living and working in the care sector during the pandemic;
•    how limiting visits to care homes affected residents and their loved ones; and
•    how decisions to discharge patients to free up hospital beds affected those living and
     working in care homes.

The module also examines the steps taken in care homes to stop the virus spreading and
the care sector’s capacity to respond to the pandemic.

Future reports will focus on the following areas:

•    Module 7 - Test, trace and isolate

•    Module 8 - Children and young people

•    Module 9 - The economic response to the pandemic

•    Module 10 - The impact on society




1 Including care funded by the public and care paid for privately by the person or family arranging it. The type of care includes help
with personal tasks and nursing care.

Module 6: Care Sector
The Covid-19 pandemic had a devastating impact on the many millions of people across the
UK who receive adult social care. The impact was also damaging to the millions more who
provided that care, whether paid or unpaid.

Protecting the adult social care sector as a whole appeared to be something of an
afterthought. The initial focus of all four governments of the UK was on protecting hospitals.
This came at the expense of people using and providing care services, despite the
vulnerability of recipients of care. There was no “protective ring” around care homes “right
from the start” of the pandemic, as claimed at the time and a continuing lack of significant
action taken to protect care homes across the UK lasted until mid April 2020.

In care homes, there were over 43,000 recorded deaths involving Covid-19 between March
2020 and July 2022; Covid-19 was the second leading cause of death in care homes in all
four nations of the UK in 2020.

 Key findings
•    The adult social care system is made up of many different parts. This meant that
     government ministers could not easily see what was going on or control what happened.
     The action taken by the UK government, Scottish Government, Welsh Government and
     Northern Ireland Executive to try and protect workers and users of care came too late.

•    In March 2020, the four governments took the decision to speed up the discharge of
     patients from hospitals to free up much-needed hospital beds. But the lack of available
     tests meant that patients were discharged from hospital to care homes without knowing
     whether they had Covid-19.

•    At this stage, there was also no vaccine. To stop Covid-19 spreading in care homes, it
     was important to keep people separate and isolate them from others when they
     returned from hospital in case they had been infected. However, this was not initially
     included in official guidance in March 2020 in England, Wales and Northern Ireland. This
     was a significant oversight.

•    Guidance on preventing infection introduced at the beginning of the pandemic advised
     care workers to avoid being within two metres of the person being cared for. This was
     unrealistic - most social care involves close contact while helping with daily activities
     such as washing, dressing and eating.

•    From mid-March 2020, care workers were advised to wear personal protective
     equipment (PPE). However, there was a severe shortage of PPE supplies. This meant
     that care workers had to work in either unsuitable or poor-quality PPE or with no PPE at
     all. This was unacceptable.

•    Because visitors to care homes could have Covid-19 unknowingly and infect residents
     and staff, visits were very limited. The strictness and length of limits on visits caused
     enormous harm to residents and their loved ones, often resulting in a significant decline
     in the health and wellbeing of residents.

•    Supporting care workers to stay away from their jobs if they were infected with Covid-19
     was also a vital way to stop it spreading. Given their extremely important role, financial
     support to protect care workers from losing wages needs to be in place at the start of
     any pandemic.

•    Routine in-person inspections of care settings stopped, pausing important checks on
     the quality of care being provided to vulnerable residents.

•    During the pandemic, there were approximately 5.8 million unpaid carers across the UK.
     Some support was made available to them during the pandemic but it came too late and
     problems identifying unpaid carers made it difficult to deliver support.

•    In Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, registers of care workers meant that recently
     retired care professionals could be easily identified and invited to return to work. These
     registers also made it easier to communicate with the workers to signpost advice and
     guidance. There is currently no workforce register in England.

•    Data gaps were a significant problem, particularly in the early months of the pandemic.
     Differences in the way that data about deaths were collected and published meant that
     real time data on the number of deaths in care homes in each nation were not available
     until mid to late April 2020.

•    There was also a lack of data about the number of people receiving care in their own
     homes who had died. The data that are available suggest that more people died at
     home during the pandemic than before the pandemic. As healthcare professionals were
     not always available to provide support, more end-of-life care had to be provided by
     people who were untrained, including sometimes by family and friends, without the
     necessary medication and without appropriate support.

•    The speed of the pandemic meant that sensitive discussions about advance care plans
     (which record someone’s preferences for their future medical care in case they become
     seriously ill) were often rushed or did not happen at all. Before the pandemic, UK health
     and social care professionals were not required to have end-of-life training. Mandatory
     end-of-life care training is needed.

•    The Inquiry heard concerns about Do Not Attempt Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation
     (DNACPR) decisions not being made appropriately or sensitively. A DNACPR is a
     decision by medical staff not to attempt CPR. People can also state their own wishes
     about this in advance. DNACPRs are person specific and should not be applied to
     groups of people. Discussions about DNACPR must be handled sensitively and in a way
     that suits each person.

Recommendations
The recommendations in this Report must be acted upon now before the next pandemic
strikes. In order to ensure that the UK’s adult social care sector is better protected and
equipped to respond to the next pandemic, the Inquiry recommends:


     improving arrangements for pandemic planning to ensure clearer structures, roles
     and responsibilities;


     better planning for separating (isolating) care home residents with potential infections,
     including those discharged from hospitals;


     implementing financial support schemes that are ready to be rolled out to support
     adult social care staff to self-isolate/limit staff movement;

     publishing guidance on how limiting visiting will be decided upon in a future
     pandemic;

     maintaining inspection and oversight by developing protocols to enable inspections
     to continue, including, where possible, on-site inspections;


     improving collection of data about deaths of workers in the adult social care sector;


     establishing mandatory end-of-life training for relevant health and social care workers;
     and

     recognising and supporting the social care workforce in England through the
     introduction of a voluntary register.


A full list of the Inquiry’s recommendations is included in Appendix 3 of the full report. The
Chair expects that all recommendations are acted upon and implemented. The Inquiry will
monitor the implementation of the recommendations during its lifetime.
