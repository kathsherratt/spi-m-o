Evaluating SPI-M-O

Notes
Published

March 1, 2026

Graham Medley summarises SPI-M-O’s consensus process as performing three “invaluable” functions:

  • peer review
  • emphasis of uncertainty
  • shared responsibility

While Medley’s view is strictly positive, I think each of these also hold risks as well as benefits. I also think these could in fact be evaluated, at least to some degree. These functions also prompts some quite specific questions for the design of new advisory structures.

As a side note, and a rationale for using these three functions rather than some other: I think this three-part structure maps nicely to the “Quality”, “Value”, and “Trust” framework used for evaluating official statistics (see: Office for Statistics Regulation). (I’m aware SPI-M-O certainly does not produce official statistics. But I don’t think that should stop us from holding the work up to the same level of scrutiny just to see what falls out.)

Here are some notes and sketches on how evaluation could proceed, based on the “after action review” format.

peer review

What was supposed to happen?

SPI-M-O as a group is motivated by the idea that comparison from multiple sources of expertise both provides quality assurance and improves overall quality. This has changed form over multiple national crises that have leaned on modelling work. For example, during H1N1 SPI-M-O participants were tasked with critiquing a single model owned by the Health Protection Agency, rather than providing primary modelling work (see: history).

This process also echoes structured elicitation methods, that are designed to provide multiple comparison while mitigating the biases that group dynamics are prone to (e.g. anchoring, halo effects). (Notably, the UK government already uses SEE protocols in the Government Office for Science Foresight team (the “Futures playbook”).)

Given the intended assurance and improvement of quality, policy audiences might reasonably expect that SPI-M-O work at least matches the quality standard expected of central government analysis (the “Aqua Book”).

What actually happened?

In practice, peer review meant independent teams each contributing estimates through their own methods, then comparing them in group discussion. Fairly little of this has been formally evaluated as far as I know. Funk (2021) evaluated short-term forecasts in 2020. Some groups have published retrospective evaluations of their own work (Keeling 2023).

Group outputs focused on the consensus statements. These are accessible in the government archives for Covid-19 and for 2009 H1N1 (see Sources). From individual modelling contributors: where participants submitted background papers, these are published alongside the consensus statements. Individual modelling outputs are otherwise not readily accessible, but should in theory exist (so could be requested) where they were submitted to the Secretariat (weekly Rt estimates, short and medium term incidence projections).

In terms of the process: we have only patchily documented the recruitment, contribution, facilitation, and process for synthesis of the group. There are some various retrospectives published by the participants involved. Otherwise the major source of evidence for this is the COVID-19 Inquiry, and previously the H1N1 Inquiry.

What should happen?

  • What is the explicit function of SPI-M-O collaboration and/or review? Is the group reviewing each other’s work equally (peers), or reviewing a target analysis (champion/challenger)? How is work compared?
  • Could/should formal expert elicitation protocols (which generally cover recruitment, facilitation, and synthesis) be adapted to the SPI-M-O context?

emphasis of uncertainty

What was supposed to happen?

What actually happened?

SPI-M-O synthesised expert estimates into a single consensus statement produced by a non-academic secretariat. It reported only the range of Rt (a 90% CI of the mean of all contributing estimates), never a point estimate, which was a deliberate choice to convey uncertainty.

  • Comparison with retrospective best estimates. For example: even under the most favourable comparison, around 19% of consensus estimates did not correctly identify the direction of Rt (growth versus decline), relative to retrospective best-estimates using EpiEstim (McCabe 2024).

What should happen?

  • what are the most policy-relevant uncertainties, and how suited is the consensus process for addressing them?
  • how are estimates of likelihood versus estimates of impact (the two dimensions of risk) handled?
  • where does the group sit among the three lines of risk management, and is that position agreed, or assumed implictly?

shared responsibility

What was supposed to happen?

Let’s consider the UK Government framework for allocating responsibility for risk management (the Orange Book). This is applied across the whole of government activity:

  1. Operational risk management (i.e. government department)
  2. Internal review and oversight (internal audit)
  3. Independent review

What actually happened?

First, I would be curious to hear where SPI-M-O participants, intepreters, users, and the public would place SPI-M-O on the three lines. My own suspicion is that SPI-M is nominally Line 3 (independent review); when operationalised as SPI-M-O, in practice aligns closer to Line 2; and at times moved toward Line 1, by directly guiding policy (rhetorically, and in some cases actually) via the headline R estimates.

Second, are the SPI-M consensus statements perceived as reporting on risk (overall, or specifically epidemiological risk)? If so, we could assess the statement text against standards for risk reporting, i.e. the Orange Book: Good Practice Guide for Risk Reporting.

What should happen?

  • how should infrastructure and accountability change when SPI-M takes on the “SPI-M-O” operational role, rather than advisory?
  • Group advisory settings carry moral hazards, specifically the ‘diffusion of responsibility’ where no single actor is clearly accountable for the group output - how could this be protected against?
  • what institutional support exists for members during and after operationalisation?

The government has also previously published guidance on ethical research in emergencies. It would be useful to reflect on how these apply to SPI-M-O. Specifically:

Responsibilities that arise for policy-makers include support for emergency preparedness encompassing…

  • sustainable support for, and enablement of, the infrastructure required to conduct that research, including multi-institution research platforms, priority-setting mechanisms, community engagement plans, and data sharing arrangements
  • a minimum commitment to ensuring that evidence is generated during or after policy implementation, and that well-designed studies begin as soon as possible after the start of the emergency, addressing evidence gaps that have been prioritised in a transparent and accountable manner
  • transparency with respect to how emerging evidence has been incorporated into decisions (including decisions not to act), and what underlying values have steered policy and operational choices
